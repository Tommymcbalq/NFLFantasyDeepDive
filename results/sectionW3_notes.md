## §W3.1 (L6.1) Lineup-marginal value, generalised

### The quantity, and why raw value is not it

§R established the point at one pick: a player who would not start adds nothing, so the
best available RB was worth **exactly 0.000** to a roster holding 3 RB and 5 WR against
6 startable RB/WR slots. That was a demonstration, not a mechanism. L6.1 asks for the
mechanism at every roster state.

Board values are VORP-scaled — PPG above a positional replacement level — so an *unfilled*
starting slot is worth exactly 0 (a freely available replacement body fills it), and a
sub-replacement player is never started ahead of that body. Hence

$$L(\mathcal{R}) = \max_{\text{feasible assignments}} \sum_{\text{slots}} \max(v_{\text{assigned}},\,0).$$

**This floor is a correction to §R's operator, and it is stated as one.** §R summed the best
players with no floor. On a saturated roster that makes no difference. On an empty roster it
does: without the floor, drafting a negative-VORP tight end would appear to *reduce* lineup
value, which is false — you simply would not start him.

### The marginal in closed form

The slot structure — QB, TE, two RB, two WR, two FLEX open to RB and WR — is a transversal
matroid, so the optimal lineup is greedy and adding one player either enters the lineup,
displacing the weakest entrant it can reach, or does not. Therefore

$$\boxed{\ \Delta_p(v;\mathcal{R}) \;=\; \max\big(0,\ v - c_p(\mathcal{R})\big)\ }\tag{W3.2}$$

with the displacement threshold $c_p$ equal to:

- $p \in \{\text{QB},\text{TE}\}$: the incumbent starter's value, floored at 0.
- $p \in \{\text{RB},\text{WR}\}$: the **weakest of the six RB/WR-block entrants** (2 RB slots,
  2 WR slots, 2 FLEX), floored at 0. It is the whole block and not just the RB slots because
  an added RB pushes an incumbent down the RB slots and into the FLEX, and the FLEX is shared
  with WR — so the cascade from an RB addition can end by evicting a wide receiver.
- If any eligible slot is still empty, $c_p = 0$ and the marginal is the player's full value.

(W3.2) makes the marginal $O(1)$ rather than an $O(n\log n)$ re-solve, which is what makes
the rollout and the flat-versus-step identity below affordable at all.

### Beyond one step: the value function, and where myopia is wrong

$\Delta$ is a one-step quantity. The decision-theoretic object at pick $t$ is

$$V_t(\mathcal{R}) = \max_j\ \mathbb{E}\big[\,\text{score}(\mathcal{R}_{\text{final}})\ \big|\ \text{take } j \text{ now, play } \pi \text{ thereafter}\big] \tag{W3.3}$$

approximated by **rollout**: take $j$, play the myopic-marginal policy to the end against the
same simulated room, score the finished roster under the outcome model of §W3.4, average.
Rollout is one step of policy improvement, so in expectation it is no worse than the policy
it rolls out. The `rollout_gain` column reports the change in final score from forcing a
position at that pick against letting the myopic policy choose (SE from 40 states):

| pick | QB | RB | TE | WR |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | −1.82 ± .67 | −0.23 ± .46 | −3.76 ± .51 | 0.00 |
| 16 | +0.18 ± .12 | **+2.45 ± .60** | −1.93 ± .56 | 0.00 |
| 25 | +0.08 ± .20 | +0.44 ± .43 | −1.16 ± .56 | −2.26 ± .48 |
| 36 | −3.17 ± .52 | +0.01 ± .18 | −1.34 ± .69 | −2.31 ± .51 |
| 45 | −1.28 ± .29 | **+2.57 ± .44** | +0.25 ± .51 | −0.52 ± .27 |
| 56 | +0.05 ± .26 | **+2.11 ± .56** | +0.94 ± .53 | +0.03 ± .17 |

**The anomaly worth chasing is the RB column.** Forcing an RB where the myopic policy would
not is worth +2.4 to +2.6 points at picks 16, 45 and 56 — five to six standard errors. A
policy that maximises the *expected* marginal lineup value is systematically leaving RB value
on the table. Chasing it down:

The myopic marginal treats RB and WR symmetrically — they share the threshold $c_{\text{RB}}
= c_{\text{WR}}$ by (W3.2), because they compete for the same six slots. Since our board's WR
values dominate at matched draft cost, the myopic policy takes wide receivers, finishing with
**2 RB and 10 WR**. But the scorer fills a lineup *each week*, and two RB slots must be filled
every week from a two-man RB room. When one is out — availability runs 0.83 — the slot takes
replacement, worth 0. Ten wide receivers can never be short. **Expected-value marginal
analysis cannot see week-level hole risk, because it evaluates one lineup rather than
seventeen.**

This is §M's "roster-mix concavity" (points peak at 4–5 RB, falling 41–44 points at 2 and at
8 RB) *derived* rather than observed, and it is the mechanism behind the strategy results in
§W3.5–6. It also gives $\delta_{RB}$ a rationalisation that owes nothing to preference: a
structural RB premium is a first-order patch on a myopic expected-value board whose RB room
is thin. Recorded as an explanation, not as an endorsement — see §W3.7.

## §W3.2 (L6.2) The flat-versus-step principle, formalised

§R stated it in words: *flat tiers are safe to wait on however fast they drain; steps are
not, however slowly.* Here is the algebra.

### Derivation

At the owner's pick $t_0$ with next pick $t_1$, order the available position-$p$ players by
lineup-marginal value $m_{(1)} \ge m_{(2)} \ge \dots$ (marginals taken with respect to the
**same** roster state, so the two sides of the comparison are commensurable — this is
§R eq. 44.5 generalised). Define the tier gaps $\delta_i = m_{(i)} - m_{(i+1)} \ge 0$ and let
$N_p$ be the number of position-$p$ players the room removes between $t_0$ and $t_1$. The
cost of waiting is lineup-VONA,

$$W_p \;=\; m_{(1)} \;-\; \mathbb{E}\Big[\max_{j \in \mathcal{A}_{t_1},\,\text{pos}(j)=p} m_j\Big].\tag{W3.4}$$

Suppose the room removes $p$-players from the top of the $p$-list downward (*order
consistency*). Then the best $p$ available at $t_1$ is $m_{(1+N_p)}$, and the gaps telescope:

$$m_{(1)} - m_{(1+N_p)} \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{N_p} \delta_i \;=\; \sum_{i \ge 1} \delta_i\,\mathbb{1}[N_p \ge i].$$

Taking expectations,

$$\boxed{\ W_p \;=\; \sum_{i \ge 1} \mathbb{E}\big[\delta_i\,\mathbb{1}[N_p \ge i]\big]\ }\tag{W3.5}$$

**The cost of waiting is the survival-weighted sum of the tier gaps.** That single line is
the principle. Read the two factors:

- The **drain** enters only through the indicators $\mathbb{1}[N_p \ge i]$, whose expectations
  live in $[0,1]$. It is **bounded**.
- The **steps** enter linearly through $\delta_i$. They are **unbounded**.

Two corollaries, both tight:

$$\textbf{flat:}\quad \delta_i \le \varepsilon \ \ \forall i \le N_p \ \Longrightarrow\ W_p \le \varepsilon\,\mathbb{E}[N_p]$$
$$\textbf{step:}\quad W_p \ \ge\ \mathbb{E}\big[\delta_1 \mathbb{1}[N_p \ge 1]\big] \ \approx\ \delta_1 \Pr(N_p \ge 1)$$

A tier can empty arbitrarily fast and still cost at most $\varepsilon$ per body drained. A
single step of size $\delta_1$ costs $\delta_1 \Pr(N_p \ge 1)$, which is bounded away from
zero as soon as the position drains *at all*. **So the wait/take decision is first-order in
the step size and only bounded in the decay rate** — which is what §R observed and could not
justify.

### Order violation is measured, not assumed

(W3.5) is exact only under order consistency, and this room is not order-consistent: §R
measured it taking the maximum-value available player just **10.3%** of the time. So both
sides are computed from the same simulation — the identity is accumulated *inside* each run,
retaining $\mathrm{Cov}(\delta_i, \mathbb{1}[N_p \ge i])$, so the residual isolates order
violation alone — and reported as `residual` in
`results/sectionW3_flat_vs_step.csv`.

### The demonstration

`results/sectionW3_flat_vs_step.csv`, 1,200 runs per pick pair. The pair that makes the point:

| $t_0 \to t_1$ | pos | $m_{(1)}$ | $\delta_1$ | $\mathbb{E}[N_p]$ | drain rate | $\Pr(N_p\ge1)$ | **$W_p$** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 25 → 36 | **WR** | 9.33 | **0.125** | **6.40** | 0.582/pick | 1.000 | **0.119** |
| 25 → 36 | **QB** | 9.59 | **2.092** | **0.52** | 0.048/pick | 0.507 | **1.148** |

**WR drains 12× faster than QB over the same eleven picks and costs 10× less to wait on.**
Both positions have essentially the same best-available marginal (9.33 vs 9.59), so the
comparison is not confounded by level. The entire difference is $\delta_1$: 0.125 against
2.092. That is the principle in one row-pair, and it is the opposite of what the drain rate
alone would tell you.

The same contrast appears again and again down the board. At 5 → 16, TE has $\delta_1 = 0.67$
but $\mathbb{E}[N] = 0.00$, so $W = 0.000$ — nobody in this room takes a tight end that early,
and the wait is free. At 16 → 25, RB drains 3.31 bodies but $\delta_1 = 0.072$ (a genuinely
flat shelf) and $W = 0.080$. At 36 → 45, TE drains only 0.65 bodies but $\delta_1 = 1.49$ and
$W = 1.134$ — **the slowest-draining position at that pick is the second most expensive to
wait on.**

As an illustration (not as the proof — the proof is (W3.5)), regressing $W_p$ on the two
candidate explanations across all 52 (pick-pair, position) cells:

| specification | coefficient on $\delta_1$ | coefficient on $\mathbb{E}[N_p]$ | $R^2$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| step only | +0.486 ± 0.102 | — | 0.311 |
| decay only | — | +0.129 ± 0.046 | 0.134 |
| both | **+0.528 ± 0.089** | +0.152 ± 0.036 | 0.496 |

Both enter, as (W3.5) says they must — the cost is a *product*, not a horse race. But the
step term carries more than twice the explanatory weight, and, decisively, the step
coefficient is what makes the cost *large*: $\delta_1$ ranges over 0.02–2.35 in these cells
while $\mathbb{E}[N_p]$ ranges over 0–6.8 with a coefficient a third the size. n = 52 cells
from one board and one opponent model; the regression is descriptive.

The residuals of (W3.5) are negative wherever the room's ordering diverges most from ours —
largest at RB 16→25 (−1.55) and WR 16→25 (−1.69), where the identity predicts a cost of
~1.6 and the realised cost is ~0.05, because the players this room removes are **not** the
top of our marginal list. That is §R's 10.3% expressed as points, and it means the identity
is an **upper bound** on the cost of waiting in a room that does not share your board.

## §W3.3 (L7.1) Expected order from the owner's beliefs, and availability overrides

### The generative model

Pre-registered form. Player $j$ carries a latent draft time

$$z_j = \mu_j + d_j + \varepsilon_j,\qquad \varepsilon_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_j^2),$$

and opponents consume players in ascending $z$.

- $\mu_j$ is the **owner's stated expected pick** for the 28 players he ordered
  (`results/expected_order_2026.csv`), and translated ADP beyond that block. It is not ADP
  inside the block: his order and ADP disagree materially — James Cook is his 9th and ADP's
  16th, Kenneth Walker his 14th and ADP's 26th, Drake London his 24th and ADP's 10th.
- $\sigma_j$ inside the block is fixed by the mock's **mean absolute slot error of 1.40** over
  picks 7–26. The naive conversion $\sigma = 1.40\sqrt{\pi/2} = 1.755$ treats the error as a
  latent draw, but the measured quantity is a *rank* error, and ranks are order statistics of
  a jointly drawn vector. So $\sigma$ is solved numerically so the **simulated** mean
  $|\text{rank} - \mu|$ equals 1.40: the solution is $\sigma = 1.775$ (scale 1.012), i.e. the
  naive value was right to within 1%. Reported because the check was run, not because it moved
  anything. *(An earlier version of this diagnostic averaged ranks before differencing, which
  measures bias rather than error and is near zero for any symmetric noise; the 1.10 figure it
  produced was an artefact of that and is withdrawn.)*
- $\sigma_j$ beyond the block is the FFC across-draft SD of the player's own slot. Simulated
  slot SD 9.97 against FFC 9.86, correlation **0.998** across the tail — the noise model
  reproduces the dispersion it was set to.
- $d_j$ is the override shift, zero unless the owner has stated a belief.

### Overrides as constraints, not as edits — the mechanism

The owner has stated availabilities that are not model output: Gibbs, Bijan and Chase are
**0%** at pick 5, Puka Nacua is "low", Drake London is **0%** in the third, Chris Olave is
**~20%**. These are hard constraints on the survival distribution.

The wrong way to impose them is to overwrite the survival table. Survival events are
**competing risks on one shared pool**: exactly one player leaves per pick. Setting
$S_{\text{London}}(25) := 0$ in an output table leaves the pick that took him unspent, and the
result is not a draft — the other survival probabilities are then jointly incoherent with it.

So the belief is imposed on the *generative* model. For an owner-stated marginal $q_j$ at his
pick $k$, solve for the latent shift $d_j$ satisfying

$$S_j(k;\,d_j) \;=\; q_j. \tag{W3.6}$$

$S_j(k;\cdot)$ is monotone increasing in $d$ — moving a player later in the queue can only
raise his survival — so the root is unique and bisection converges. Common random numbers are
held fixed across the bisection so $S(\cdot;d)$ is a deterministic function of $d$ rather than
a noisy one. Overrides interact (forcing London out early spends an opponent pick that would
otherwise have taken somebody else), so the roots are cycled to a fixed point; convergence
here took four cycles, max coordinate movement falling 11.21 → 1.46 → 0.03 → 0.00. Achieved
marginals are reported against targets.

**Rejected alternative.** Keep the model untouched and importance-weight simulated paths by
the indicator that all overrides hold. That is unbiased and preserves the joint exactly, but
under 2% of paths satisfy these six constraints jointly and the effective sample size
collapses. The location shift keeps every path usable and matches the stated marginals
exactly; **what it does not preserve is the model's joint dependence among the overridden
players**, and that is the price, stated.

## §W3.4 The outcome model, and the circularity it exists to avoid

A prospective strategy comparison scored on *expected lineup value under the board* is
**circular**: the lineup-marginal greedy maximises exactly that quantity at every step, so it
wins by construction and the comparison carries no information. §M avoided this by scoring
rosters on realised weekly points under a weekly-optimal lineup. The prospective analogue,
built only from quantities already on the board:

$$X_j = \texttt{final}_j + \texttt{post\_SD\_bl}_j\, z_j,\quad z_j \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)
\qquad a_{j,w} \sim \text{Bernoulli}(\texttt{avail}_j)$$

Each of 17 weeks, fill 1QB/2RB/2WR/1TE/2FLEX from the players *present* that week, every slot
floored at 0; score is the mean over weeks, so the units stay §R's PPG. Bench depth now has
genuine value because starters miss weeks, and the greedy's objective is no longer the scoring
function. A declared sensitivity adds week-to-week scoring noise
$e_{j,w}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2_{\text{tier}})$; it is reported separately because
$\sigma^2_{\text{tier}}$ is absent for QB and TE on the board and has to be imputed, so it is
an assumption rather than a measurement.

**The limit this does not escape, stated plainly.** The board is both the policy input and the
scoring truth. So a strategy that follows the board more closely beats one that does not, *by
construction*, and the prospective arm therefore **cannot** test whether the board is right or
whether it beats ADP. It can only compare strategies that share the board — sequencing, roster
construction, position timing, the $\delta_{RB}$ tilt. **Everything in §W3.5 is conditional on
the board being correct.** The unconditional question is §W3.6's, and only real outcomes can
answer it.

## §W3.6 (L7.3) Does §M's "draft the board" verdict survive?

### The retrospective arm — the one that can answer it

`scripts/72_sectionW3_m_rerun.py`. §M's design, unchanged where it can be: 2015–2024, 10
slots, snake, 14 rounds, scored on **realised weekly PPR** under a weekly-optimal lineup

### Chasing it: why do they lose, and is it the board or the sequencer?

The roster mixes give it away immediately. SA (ADP) ends with **5.0 RB / 5.8 WR**; SB and SD
end with **2.0 RB / 10.0 WR**. §M1 explains the mechanism: because the FLEX is open to both RB
and WR, the two replacement levels are forced to a common cutoff ($R_{RB} = 170.1$,
$R_{WR} = 169.8$), after which VORP ordering collapses to raw points ordering, which favours
WRs at every matched rank (§L: +11.7 → +42.3). So best-available-by-VORP is not a strategy;
it is a roster-construction failure with a value board attached. This is the same defect
§W3.1's rollout exposed, arriving from historical data instead of simulation.

Two **diagnostics** were added after seeing that, for that diagnosed structural reason. They
are family = 0, excluded from the BH family, and reported exactly as §M handled its own
post-hoc diagnostics. **SF** = SB with a minimum of 4 RB by round 7. **SG** = SF + $\delta_{RB}$.

| opponents | strategy | Δ vs SA | SE | t | p | MDE | seasons > 0 | roster mix |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| logit | **SF** board + min-4-RB | **−4.2** | 15.9 | −0.26 | .799 | 44.5 | 5/10 | 1.8/4.0/6.6/1.6 |
| logit | SG SF + δ_RB | −37.8 | 15.1 | −2.51 | .033 | 42.2 | 1/10 | 1.6/6.2/4.8/1.4 |

| adp | SG | −90.9 | 24.7 | −3.67 | .005 | 69.3 | 1/10 | 1.2/7.0/4.1/1.8 |

**The board's ordering is not the problem; its roster mix was the whole problem.** Constrain
the RB count and the −118 becomes −4.2 ± 15.9, indistinguishable from ADP, 5 seasons out of 10
positive. The board buys nothing over ADP once roster construction is fixed — but it costs
nothing either, which is a materially different statement from "the model board loses 118
points a season."

**And $\delta_{RB}$ makes it worse once roster construction is already handled:** SG is −37.8
against SF's −4.2 under behavioural opponents, and −90.9 against −16.1 under ADP opponents.
Two readings, and they are not exclusive:

- *Double-correction.* $\delta_{RB}$ and a minimum-RB rule are two patches on the same defect.
  Applied together they overshoot — under ADP opponents SG drafts 7.0 RB against SA's 5.0, past
  the peak of §M's concavity curve.
- *Paying a premium the room already pays.* Under behavioural opponents SG's mix (6.2 RB) is
  the *same* as SA's (6.2 RB) and it still loses 37.8 points, so mix cannot be the whole story
  there. What is left is *which* RBs: the fitted room already pays $\alpha_{RB} = +1.66$ logits
  — about +1.5 PPG — over our board for a running back. Bidding RB up further in a room that
  already bids RB up means paying the premium twice. §38(3) required that any edge here come
  from opponents being *predictably biased*; the measured bias is pro-RB, so the profitable
  response to it is anti-RB, and $\delta_{RB}$ points the other way.

### What the behavioural opponents changed

The ADP null scores **1,906.9** points against logit opponents and **1,738.2** against ADP
opponents — **+169 season points, harvestable by doing nothing but drafting ADP.** That is
what a room that overpays +1.7 logits for RB and +2.0 for TE leaves on the table. It is the
largest number in this section, and it accrues to the *null*: the edge against this room is in
the room's own bias, and no strategy studied here captures more of it than simply not sharing
the bias. But note that the opponent model is transported from a single 2026 draft onto ten
historical seasons; the +169 is a modelled quantity, not a measured one.

## §W3.7 $\delta_{RB}$: three things that must be reconciled

Reported together because they point in different directions and the discrepancy is not
resolved.

**(a) It may be a repair of a known board defect, not a preference.** The L5.1 units fix should
raise WR replacement more than RB replacement, shifting the board in RB's favour by an amount
of the same order as $\delta_{RB}$ itself. If so, his intuition is compensating — approximately
correctly — for an error already identified in the board, and **applying both the fix and
$\delta_{RB}$ counts the correction twice.** *This was written as a prediction before
`board_2026_v2.csv` existed; §W3.9 tests it against the delivered board and it is confirmed.*

**(b) Its identified interval does not identify a strategy.** Sweeping $\delta_{RB}$ on common
random numbers (`results/sectionW3_delta_rb_sensitivity.csv`, paired SEs ≈ 0.14):

| $\delta_{RB}$ | 0.00 | 0.50 | 1.00 | **1.29** | **1.345** | **1.40** | 1.75 | 2.00 | 2.50 | 3.00 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Δ vs ADP null | 0.00 | +0.45 | +1.03 | **+1.78** | **+0.65** | **+2.02** | +0.30 | −1.24 | −4.09 | −6.31 |

The function is **jagged, and reproducibly so** — the 1.29 → 1.345 → 1.40 swings are 8σ against
their paired Monte-Carlo error, on identical opponent draws. Tracing the divergence: 163 of 300
paired runs differ, and **all of the systematic difference is at pick 1**. At
$\delta_{RB} = 1.29$ CMC's tilted value is 13.467 against Amon-Ra's 13.469 and the policy takes
Amon-Ra; at 1.345 it is 13.522 and the policy takes CMC. The revealed-preference estimate sits
*exactly on* the discontinuity, because the choice that identified it is the choice that the
discontinuity is made of. **A parameter identified at a knife edge cannot pin the value of a
policy that is discontinuous at that knife edge.** This is §21's weak identification — a 7.0
PPG market-implied spread against 1.7–2.9 PPG per-player uncertainty — showing up as chaos in a
deterministic argmax policy. It is a reason to prefer smooth policies (or randomised tie-breaks
over the posterior) to argmax, and that is a design question for WS2's board, not for WS3.

**(c) Historically it loses.** §W3.6: SC and SE lose under both opponent models, and SG loses
against SF. On real 2015–2024 outcomes, tilting toward RB on top of a value board is worse than
not doing it. The one defence is (a): if the tilt is repairing the units defect then the
historical test — which uses §M's own replacement levels, not the defective ones — has nothing
for it to repair, and it is pure over-correction there. That defence is testable and untested.

## §W3.8 What §W3 establishes, and what it does not

**Establishes.**
1. Lineup-marginal value has a closed form (W3.2) and the correction it applies is exactly the
   incumbent starter's value at the position — zero before a slot is filled, discontinuous
   after. Generalising §R's single observation to every state.
2. The flat-versus-step principle is an identity, (W3.5): the cost of waiting is the
   survival-weighted sum of the tier gaps. Decay enters bounded, steps enter linearly. At
   pick 25 → 36, WR drains 12× faster than QB and costs 10× less to wait on, at nearly
   identical best-available value.
3. Owner availability beliefs can be imposed as hard constraints on a competing-risks survival
   model by calibrating a latent location shift (W3.6), preserving the draft's combinatorial
   structure. Achieved marginals match targets to ≤0.002.
4. §M's verdict **survives** the addition of behavioural opponents, $\delta_{RB}$, and
   lineup-marginal value: 0 of 4 pre-registered strategies beat the ADP null and all 4 lose
   significantly under BH q = 0.10 with DM season clusters. The mechanism is roster mix, and
   the family = 0 diagnostic isolates it: constrain RB count and the gap collapses to
   −4.2 ± 15.9.
5. An expected-value lineup-marginal policy systematically under-drafts RB, because it prices
   one lineup rather than seventeen and cannot see week-level hole risk at a two-slot position
   with a two-man room. §M's roster-mix concavity, derived.

**Does not establish.**
1. That the model board beats ADP. The prospective arm is circular by construction (§W3.4) and
   the retrospective arm says the board is a **wash** with ADP once roster construction is
   fixed, not a win.
2. Anything about $\delta_{RB}$'s validity. Three readings are open (§W3.7) and they conflict.
3. Anything per-manager. §38(2)'s τ-persistence pre-test still cannot be run — one draft, 87
   opponent choices — so no per-manager layer, no spike-and-slab affinity, no meta×profile
   interaction. This is unchanged from §R and remains blocked on a second logged draft.
4. That the +169 points against a biased room is real. It is the fitted 2026 room transported
   onto ten historical seasons; the transport is an extrapolation, and the control arm exists
   precisely so the reader can see how much of every result rides on it.
5. Any QB timing conclusion. Blocked on WS2's L5.1 replacement units fix — the defect at QB is
   3.17 points against a QB spread smaller than that.

**Standard errors, and what is not in them.** Every prospective SE is Monte-Carlo error of one
model. It contains outcome uncertainty (board posterior SD, availability) and opponent-order
uncertainty. It does **not** contain board bias, error in the fitted opponent model (n = 78
choices from one draft), or error in the owner's stated order. The retrospective SEs are DM
across 10 season clusters and do contain real outcome variation, which is why they are the ones
quoted for the §M verdict. **No point estimate here should be read without the qualifier that
the opponent model comes from a single realisation of a single draft.**

## §W3.9 Robustness on `board_2026_v2.csv` — and the resolution of $\delta_{RB}$

*Added after WS2 delivered `results/board_2026_v2.csv`. Everything above was written first,
against `board_2026.csv`; the §W3.7(a) prediction and the §W3.6 QB caveat were both recorded
before this board existed. Re-run verbatim with
`--board results/board_2026_v2.csv --prefix sectionW3_v2`; outputs are the `sectionW3_v2_*`
family. The retrospective §M arm (§W3.6) is **unaffected** — it uses §M's own historical
replacement levels, not the 2026 board.*

### What changed in the board

The v1 → v2 change is, to three decimals, a pure per-position level shift — exactly the L5.1
replacement-units correction and nothing else reaching WS3:

| position | mean Δ`final` | SD of Δ | replacement v1 → v2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| QB | **−2.96** | 0.03 | 12.10 → 15.17 |
| TE | −1.57 | 0.18 | 7.87 → 9.79 |
| WR | −1.54 | 0.00 | 6.38 → 7.92 |
| RB | **+0.14** | 0.00 | 6.21 → 7.47 |

Within-position ordering is untouched (Pearson 0.954, Spearman 0.920 overall, both driven
entirely by cross-position reordering). The top-140 composition moves from 24 QB / 5 TE to
**16 QB / 4 TE**, against ADP's 19 QB / 13 TE — the QB inflation flagged in §W3.6 is gone.

### The $\delta_{RB}$ prediction, tested

**The net RB-over-WR shift is $+0.14 - (-1.54) = +1.68$ board points.** The owner's revealed
premium, identified from two independent choices on the *v1* board, is **1.29–1.40**. The
prediction in §W3.7(a) was that these would be of the same order; they are, and 1.68 sits just
above the interval.

The consequence is decisive. Sweeping $\delta_{RB}$ on the v2 board, same design as §W3.7(b),
paired SEs ≈ 0.12:

| $\delta_{RB}$ | 0.00 | 0.50 | 1.00 | 1.29 | 1.345 | 1.40 | 1.75 | 2.00 | 3.00 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Δ vs ADP null, v1 board | 0.00 | +0.45 | +1.03 | +1.78 | +0.65 | +2.02 | +0.30 | −1.24 | −6.31 |
| **Δ vs ADP null, v2 board** | **0.00** | **−2.13** | **−4.32** | **−5.15** | **−5.57** | **−5.78** | **−7.24** | **−7.66** | **−7.73** |

On the corrected board the function is **monotone decreasing and steep from zero** — every
$t < -24$ — and the jaggedness documented in §W3.7(b) is gone, because the near-ties that made
the argmax chaotic were themselves a product of the mis-scaled positions sitting on top of one
another. The unconstrained board policy already drafts **7.2 RB / 4.8 WR** on v2 (against
2.0 / 10.0 on v1); adding $\delta_{RB} = 1.345$ drives it to **10.0 RB / 2.0 WR**, an
unplayable roster, and costs 5.6 PPG.

**Conclusion. $\delta_{RB}$ is not a preference to be carried forward as a structural view; it
was a correct intuition about a defective board, and the defect is now fixed.** Applying it to
`board_2026_v2.csv` double-counts a correction that WS2 has already made, and the cost is
large and unambiguous. If the owner still wants an RB tilt on the corrected board, it must be
re-derived from choices made *against that board* — the 1.29 and 1.40 figures are now stale by
construction, because both were differences between v1 values that have since moved by
−1.54 (WR) and +0.14 (RB). Recomputed on v2 the same two comparisons give Amon-Ra − CMC =
−0.39 and Rice − Barkley = −0.13, i.e. **the corrected board already prefers the running back
in both cases without any tilt at all.**

This also retires the min-RB diagnostic in the prospective arm: on v2 the S8/S9 roster-count
constraints are inert (the policy already exceeds them) and their Δ collapses to zero.

### The QB-timing caveat, discharged

§W3.6 suspended the "should we have taken a quarterback there" question pending this fix. On v2:

| comparison | v1 board | v2 board |
|---|---|---|
| defer QB to round 8+ vs board policy | −0.95 PPG | **−0.18 PPG** |
| defer QB to round 12+ vs board policy | −0.83 PPG | −0.17 PPG |
| force QB with the first pick vs board policy | −2.48 PPG | −0.68 PPG |

The cost of waiting on quarterback **falls by a factor of five** once QB replacement is put on
the right basis. The v1 board's confident "take the quarterback" was, as flagged in advance,
mostly the 3.07-point units defect at QB talking. On v2 the QB timing decision is close to a
wash and should be treated as one. The named counterfactual is unchanged and if anything
sharper: removing a specific quarterback from the universe costs nothing, because the tier is
flat (§W3.2).

### What survives unchanged

- **(W3.2), (W3.5) and the override mechanism (W3.6) are board-independent** — they are
  identities and estimators, not estimates.
- **The flat-versus-step demonstration survives with different actors.** On v2 the cleanest
  pair is pick 36 → 45: **TE** drains 0.67 bodies with $\delta_1 = 1.936$ and costs
  $W = 1.421$ to wait on, while **RB** drains 2.83 bodies — 4× faster — with $\delta_1 = 0.261$
  and costs $W = 1.117$. Slowest drain, highest cost, again.
- **§W3.6's verdict is untouched.** The §M re-run never used the 2026 board.
- **§W3.1's rollout finding flips sign, exactly as the mechanism predicts.** On v1 the myopic
  policy under-drafted RB (rollout gains +2.4 to +2.6). On v2 it *over*-drafts RB and the largest
  positive rollout gain moves to **WR at pick 5 (+1.83 ± 0.46)**. The finding was never "RB is
  undervalued"; it was "a myopic expected-value marginal cannot see week-level hole risk at a
  thin position," and on v2 the thin position is the wide receiver room.
